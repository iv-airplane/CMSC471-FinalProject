City in Motion

Visualizing taxi pickups and urban flow in New York City

Over the past decade, transportation in New York City has undergone a major transformation. Traditional taxis once dominated the system, but the rapid growth of ride-hailing services has reshaped how people move throughout the city. This shift has changed when people travel, moving away from predictable commuting patterns toward a more flexible, on-demand system. While these changes are most visible over time, they are also reflected in how activity is distributed across the city today. This project explores that transformation by examining how the composition of trips has changed, how travel behavior has evolved over time, and what today’s transportation patterns look like across different parts of the city.

Team members: Ryley Haynes, Ivan Bliznikov, Dwarakesh Baraneetharan, Emily Cheng, Aadarsh Patel

Dataset

This project uses trip data from the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC Trip Record Data) . The dataset contains detailed records of taxi and for-hire vehicle trips, including pickup time, location, and vehicle type, and spans millions of trips each month.

For our analysis, we focus on a few key fields: taxi zone ID, borough, pickup date and hour, vehicle type, and total trip count.

The main vehicle types in the dataset are:

Yellow Taxis
Traditional taxis, mostly in Manhattan and at airports
Green Taxis
Traditional taxis, mostly in the outer boroughs
For-Hire Vehicles (FHV)
Pre-booked car services like limousines and black cars
High Volume FHV (HVFHV)
App-based services like Uber and Lyft

We present results in widening focus: market share by year (the long arc), then when trips happen in a recent month, then how that weekly clock shifted over a decade (same calendar month, two years), and finally where demand concentrates across the city on one day.

How did ride-hailing reshape the taxi industry in NYC?

This visualization aims to show how market share of different taxi providers changed after the rise of the ride-hailing services in NYC. The stacked bar chart displays the percentage market share of each category for every year in the dataset, making it easier to observe long-term structural changes in the industry. Key events are annotated to provide additional context.

The donut chart and legend at the top summarize a single selected year in greater detail. They show both the total number of trips recorded that year and the percentage share of each vehicle category. Selecting a year in the bar chart updates the donut chart and legend to reflect the chosen year.

Green taxis began operating in NYC in 2013. However, their market share has steadily declined since 2015, and they have never achieved popularity comparable to yellow taxis. The sharpest decline occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic: green taxi trips fell from 6.3 million in 2019 to 1.7 million in 2020, about 27% of the 2019 level (a drop of roughly 73%). By 2023, green taxis accounted for only about 787,000 trips. According to Wikipedia, TLC data showed that only 891 green taxis were still operating by February 2023, and in May 2023, the TLC began phasing out the program. The growth of app-dispatched ride-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft largely replaced the market segment that green taxis were originally designed to serve.

Despite the rapid rise of ride-hailing services, the iconic yellow taxi has remained prevalent. Since 2020, yellow taxis have maintained a relatively stable market share of around 13%. Dispatch-only car services have also retained their position, fluctuating between roughly 6% and 8% market share since 2018. Unlike street-hail taxis, this sector was less disrupted by the expansion of high-volume for-hire vehicles (HVFHV).

Events of interest

  • The 2018 ride-hailing cap (a temporary pause on issuing new HVFHV licenses to study congestion) did not significantly slow the growth of app-dispatched services; their market share still increased by about 6%.
  • As expected, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a dramatic decline in total taxi trips, dropping from approximately 368 million trips in 2019 to 184 million in 2020.
  • The rise of HVFHV services significantly disrupted the traditional taxi industry, reducing the market share of street-hail services by roughly 50% for yellow taxis and about 8% for green taxis.

What does travel look like today?

The City Wake Up heatmap uses October 2025 TLC trip records (about 28.2 million pickups across all vehicle types in that month). Each cell sums trips for one hour of the day and one day of the week; color follows a sequential yellow-to-red scale, with deeper red indicating more trips. Use the dropdown to view all types together or to isolate yellow taxi, green taxi, FHV, or high-volume FHV (app-based) trips. Hovering shows the exact weekday, hour range, and count for that cell.

City Wake Up: Total Trips by Hour and Day of Week

In October 2025, weekday demand still follows a commuting-shaped curve: volumes are lowest in the predawn hours, climb through the morning, ease somewhat mid-day, and rise again toward the evening. Weekends are flatter in the morning but stay busier late at night and into the early morning than typical weekdays, and the busiest evening hours shift slightly later.

Vehicle type splits that story further. For the month shown, high-volume FHV (app) trips peak on Friday in the early evening (around 7 p.m.), and Saturday night is also elevated in a pattern consistent with ride-hail use for going out. Yellow taxis peak slightly earlier on Friday (around 6 p.m.), aligning with end-of-work travel and street-hail demand in core areas. Together, these contrasts support the project theme: app-based services did not only substitute for taxis; they concentrate more of their volume in late-week and evening periods while yellow taxis remain tied to classic weekday peaks.

Deeper Dive: How has travel behavior shifted?

The snapshot above is October 2025 only. The view below keeps the same hour-by-weekday layout but compares October 2025 to October 2015 cell by cell (after matching vehicle-type definitions as closely as the TLC allows). Blue means fewer trips in that slot than ten years earlier; red means more. This is explicitly about change in the weekly clock, not a second “live today” view.

To understand how travel patterns evolved over time, this heatmap compares total taxi trips across all providers from October 2015 to October 2025. Blue areas represent times with fewer trips than in 2015, while red areas indicate periods with increased trip volume. Overall, most periods show higher trip counts in 2025 than in 2015.

The largest increases are concentrated during commuting hours, particularly from 6-9 AM and 4-6 PM on weekdays. Wednesday stands out as the day with the strongest growth, especially during the morning (7-9 AM) and evening (4-7 PM) commuting hours. In contrast, Saturday shows the sharpest decline in late-night activity, especially between 12 and 4 AM. While other weekdays experienced some increase in late-night trips, the growth was relatively modest.

One possible explanation is that hybrid work schedules have concentrated commuting activity into the middle of the week, increasing demand for taxis and ride-hailing services from Wednesday through Friday. At the same time, the decline in late-night Saturday trips may suggest changes in NYC nightlife and social behavior compared to 2015.

How does the city move today?

Today’s transportation system reflects these changes in behavior across both space and time. This map allows you to explore how trip activity varies throughout the day, at both the borough level and the more detailed neighborhood (taxi zone) level. It uses Wednesday, October 16, 2024 (see processed trip data for that date), so you can step through hours without averaging away geography.

At the borough level, broad patterns emerge across The Bronx, Brooklyn, Manhattan, Queens, and Staten Island. The neighborhood view adds localized hotspots and how they shift through the day.

For ride-hailing platforms and taxi operators, demand at both borough and neighborhood scale matters for allocation and service design.

In short: trip activity is highly uneven in space and time; the map makes that unevenness legible on one representative midweek day.

Summary: The Evolving Story of New York City's Transportation

Together, these visualizations tell a story of profound structural and behavioral change in how New York City moves.

The market share chart reveals the industry's transformation over the past decade. The explosive rise of high-volume for-hire vehicles (app-based services like Uber and Lyft) has fundamentally reshaped the taxi landscape. What was once dominated by yellow taxis has been largely displaced. Yellow taxis themselves dropped from roughly 60% market share in 2015 to about 13% by 2023, while HVFHV services grew from nearly nothing to over 50% of all recorded trips. This shift accelerated after 2015 and weathered the COVID-19 pandemic without slowing. Yet this is more than just a story of one provider replacing another. The decline of green taxis and the stability of dispatch-only services show that the rise of ride-hailing filled a fundamentally new market segment, not simply a direct substitution.

But market share is only half the story. The hourly heatmaps reveal how this industry shift has been accompanied by changes in *when* people travel. In October 2025, the classic weekday commute pattern persists. Morning and evening peaks remain strong, but the weekend and late-night signatures have shifted. High-volume FHV services concentrate their volume on Friday evenings and Saturday nights, indicating a concentration of leisure and nightlife trips, while yellow taxis maintain a more distributed, commute-oriented pattern. Over the decade from October 2015 to October 2025, the most striking changes have been an intensification of midweek commuting (particularly Wednesday morning and evening) and a marked decline in Saturday late-night activity. These shifts point to underlying changes in work patterns, such as hybrid schedules concentrating commutes into the middle of the week, and in social behavior, where late-night Saturday trips have become less common than a decade ago.

The geographic map grounds these temporal and market patterns in space. Trip activity is highly concentrated, with Manhattan and outer-borough hubs showing dramatic peaks and valleys throughout the day. The geography reinforces the narrative. Demand is not evenly distributed but clustered around transit nodes, commercial centers, and neighborhoods, and those clusters shift predictably through the day. Together with the hourly patterns, this geographic view confirms that today's transportation system is highly specialized. Different vehicle types serve different needs, such as yellow taxis during weekday commutes and app services during weekend nightlife, at different times in different places. What began as a disruptive technology has matured into a complementary transportation ecosystem, but one that has fundamentally altered the temporal and spatial signature of mobility in the city.

Implementation

The page is static HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. We aggregate millions of TLC rows offline with DuckDB, serve compact CSV/JSON to the browser, and build views with D3 (map and heatmaps) and Vega-Lite (market share). Taxi zones come from TLC shapefiles, simplified to GeoJSON (e.g. via Mapshaper).

Run locally: clone the repo, then from the project root run python -m http.server 8765 and open http://127.0.0.1:8765/. Regenerating processed files is documented in the repository.

Full pipeline notes, schemas, preprocessing scripts, and design rationale are in the code repository (GitHub): README.md and DATA_README.md.
