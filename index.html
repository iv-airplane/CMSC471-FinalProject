City in Motion

Visualizing taxi pickups and urban flow in New York City

Over the past decade, transportation in New York City has undergone a major transformation. Traditional taxis once dominated the system, but the rapid growth of ride-hailing services has reshaped how people move throughout the city. This shift has changed when people travel, moving away from predictable commuting patterns toward a more flexible, on-demand system. While these changes are most visible over time, they are also reflected in how activity is distributed across the city today. This project explores that transformation by examining how the composition of trips has changed, how travel behavior has evolved over time, and what today’s transportation patterns look like across different parts of the city.

Dataset

This project uses trip data from the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC Trip Record Data) . The dataset contains detailed records of taxi and for-hire vehicle trips, including pickup time, location, and vehicle type, and spans millions of trips each month.

For our analysis, we focus on a few key fields: taxi zone ID, borough, pickup date and hour, vehicle type, and total trip count.

The main vehicle types in the dataset are:

Yellow Taxis
Traditional taxis, mostly in Manhattan and at airports
Green Taxis
Traditional taxis, mostly in the outer boroughs
For-Hire Vehicles (FHV)
Pre-booked car services like limousines and black cars
High Volume FHV (HVFHV)
App-based services like Uber and Lyft

How did travel behavior change?

This visualization aims to show how market share of different taxi providers changed after the rise of the ride-hailing services in NYC. The stacked bar chart displays the percentage market share of each category for every year in the dataset, making it easier to observe long-term structural changes in the industry. Key events are annotated to provide additional context.

The donut chart and legend at the top summarizes a single selected year in greater detail.They show both the total number of trips recorded that year and the percentage share of each vehicle category. Selecting a year in the bar chart updates the donut chart and legend to reflect the chosen year.

Green taxis began operating in NYC in 2013. However, their market share has steadily declined since 2015, and they have never achieved popularity comparable to yellow taxis. The sharpest decline occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic: green taxi trips fell from 6.3 million in 2019 to 1.7 million in 2020, a 3.6x decrease. By 2023, green taxis accounted for only about 787,000 trips. According to Wikipedia, TLC data showed that only 891 green taxis were still operating by February 2023, and in May 2023, the TLC began phasing out the program. The growth of app-dispatched ride-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft largely replaced the market segment that green taxis were originally designed to serve.

Despite the rapid rise of ride-hailing services, the iconic yellow taxi has remained prevalent. Since 2020, yellow taxis have maintained a relatively stable market share of around 13%. Dispatch-only car services have also retained their position, fluctuating between roughly 6% and 8% market share since 2018. Unlike street-hail taxis, this sector was less disrupted by the expansion of high-volume for-hire vehicles (HVFHV).

Events of interest

  • The 2018 ride-hailing cap - a temporary pause on issuing new HFHV licenses to study congestion - did not significantly slow the growth of app-dispatched taxis, whose market share still increased by about 6%.
  • As expected, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a dramatic decline in total taxi trips, dropping from approximately 368 million trips in 2019 to 184 million in 2020.
  • The rise of HFHV services significantly disrupted the traditional taxi industry, reducing the market share of street-hail services by roughly 50% for yellow taxis and about 8% for green taxis.

What does travel look like today

The City Wake Up heatmap reveals when New York City travels, mapping trip activity across every hour of the day and every day of the week. Each cell encodes trip volume using a sequential yellow-to-red color scale, where darker red indicates higher demand. A dropdown allows the reader to filter by vehicle type (all types, yellow taxi, green taxi, FHV, or high-volume FHV) so that the temporal rhythms of each part of the fleet can be examined independently. A tooltip on hover provides the exact day, hour range, and trip count for any cell of interest.

City Wake Up: Total Trips by Hour and Day of Week

The heatmap reveals that trip demand in New York City follows two distinct rhythms depending on the day of the week. On weekdays, activity drops sharply in the early morning hours before rising through the morning commute, dipping slightly in the afternoon, and peaking again during the evening. Weekends tell a different story: the overnight hours remain active well into the early morning, demand builds more gradually through the day, and the evening peak arrives later and sustains longer into the night.

These patterns shift meaningfully depending on vehicle type. High-volume FHV services show their strongest activity on Friday and Saturday evenings, reflecting their dominance in leisure and nightlife travel. Yellow taxis, by contrast, remain more concentrated in the traditional weekday commuting hours, consistent with their geographic concentration in Manhattan. Together, these contrasts reinforce a broader theme of the project: ride-hailing has not simply replaced traditional taxis, but has expanded into travel patterns, such as late nights, weekends, outer boroughs, that taxis never fully served.

Deeper Dive: How has travel behavior shifted?

To understand how these patterns evolved, this difference heatmap compares 2025 to 2015. Blue areas represent times with fewer trips than in 2015, while red areas indicate increases. We observe declines during traditional weekday commuting hours, alongside increases in evening and weekend activity. This shift highlights a move away from rigid commuting patterns toward more flexible, on-demand travel behavior.

How does the city move today?

Today’s transportation system reflects these changes in behavior across both space and time. This map allows you to explore how trip activity varies throughout the day, at both the borough level and the more detailed neighborhood (taxi zone) level. At the borough level, we can observe broad patterns — such as Manhattan’s concentration of daytime activity and the stronger evening demand in outer boroughs like Brooklyn and Queens. Switching to the neighborhood view reveals finer-grained differences, highlighting specific areas where demand is especially high or changes rapidly over time.

Together, these views show that travel patterns are not uniform across the city. Instead, demand varies significantly depending on both location and time of day, reflecting a more flexible and distributed transportation system.

For ride-hailing platforms and taxi operators, understanding demand at both the borough and neighborhood level is critical for decisions such as driver allocation, pricing, and service coverage.

Together, these views illustrate that transportation activity in New York City is highly uneven across both location and time. From the borough level view, it can be observed that Manhattan has the highest amount of daytime activity because of its prominence as an economic hub, whereas outer boroughs such as Queens experience increased activity in the evening, highlighting its residential and nightlife activity.

In terms of localities, however, it becomes apparent that the demand for transportation is not uniform but occurs in certain hotspots. There are certain taxi zones where the number of trips remains fairly consistent, whereas there are other zones that fluctuate sharply according to the time of the day. It becomes apparent that there is a large level of flexibility and decentralization of transportation, reflecting the city's multifaceted nature.

It is vital for ride-hailing companies, taxi drivers, and urban planners to recognize the variations in transportation across space and time. Insights may assist policy-makers in areas such as driver dispatching, dynamic pricing, traffic control, and coverage planning.

Implementation Details

Data Processing

The TLC dataset is very large, with millions of trips recorded each month, so we first needed to process and aggregate the data before using it in our visualizations. We used DuckDB to efficiently query the raw trip-level data and generate smaller, analysis-ready datasets.

From the original data, we created several aggregated datasets:

  • Trips by taxi zone, hour, and date (for the chloropleth map)
  • Trips by day of week and hour (for the heatmap)
  • Trips by year and vehicle type (for market share trends)

One challenge we encountered was that before 2019, high-volume FHV (HVFHV) trips were not tracked separately and were grouped with other FHV trips. As ride-hailing services became more common, the data began separating these categories. To make comparisons consistent over time, we manually split FHV and HVFHV during preprocessing.

To support the spatial visualization, we used NYC taxi zone shapefiles and converted them into GeoJSON using Mapshaper so they could be rendered in the browser.

Visualization

The visualizations are built using D3 and Vega-Lite. We use a taxi zone choropleth map to show how trip activity varies across space, a heatmap to highlight patterns across time, and a stacked bar chart with a donut chart to show changes in market share over time.

We chose a choropleth map to represent spatial patterns because it allows for easy comparison across zones, and a heatmap to show dense temporal patterns without clutter. The market share visualization uses normalized stacked bars to emphasize proportions rather than absolute values.

Interactive elements such as sliders, dropdowns, and tooltips allow users to explore different time periods and vehicle types, making it easier to compare patterns and understand how transportation activity changes across the city.
