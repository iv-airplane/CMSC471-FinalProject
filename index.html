City in Motion: Visualizing Taxi Pickups and Urban Flow in New York City

CMSC 471 Final Project · Aadarsh, Dwarakesh, Emily, Ivan, Ryley

Proposal

This project explores how transportation activity changes throughout the day in New York City. We plan to build an interactive, animated map that shows taxi and ride-sharing pickup density over a 24 hour period, allowing users to move through the day and directly compare patterns between weekdays and weekends (either through a continuous 48-hour view or toggle). In addition, users will be able to jump to key moments in the day, such as lunch or evening rush hour, to see how the activity shifts at different points. The goal of this main visualization is to give a clear sense of how the city “moves” throughout the day in a way that isn't obvious from raw data.

To support this map, we'll include a couple of supporting charts to show broader trends. A layered bar chart will show the shares of trips taken by different vehicle types (yellow taxis, green taxis, and for-hire vehicles like Uber/Lyft), allowing users to compare how the market share has changed over time, notably since Uber's founding in 2009. We will also create a heatmap with hour of the day on the x-axis and day of the week on the y-axis, where color represents trip volume. This will help us highlight recurring patterns such as weekday commuting behavior versus weekend nightlife activity.

Introduction

New York City moves on four wheels. Every day, more than a million paid trips begin somewhere in the five boroughs — in a yellow cab outside Grand Central, a green Boro Taxi on Roosevelt Avenue, an Uber summoned from a Brooklyn stoop, a black car waiting outside a hotel. Each of those trips leaves a trace in the public dataset that the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC) has been publishing for over a decade. Stitched together, those traces are a remarkably honest record of how a city of 8 million people actually moves: when it commutes, where it goes out at night, which neighborhoods get served and which get skipped, and how a once-stable system was reshaped almost overnight by app-based ride-hail.

Reading that record straight from the raw files is hopeless — each month is hundreds of thousands of rows of pickup timestamps, drop-off coordinates, fare amounts, and license codes. The patterns are there, but they only become legible once you aggregate, color, and put them in motion. That is what this project is for. We use three coordinated visualizations, each at a different time scale, to turn the TLC archive into something a curious reader can actually see: the long arc of the industry since the rise of Uber and Lyft, the heartbeat of a typical week, and the geographic spread of pickups across a single ordinary day.

What we want you to be able to see

The visualizations are built around three concrete questions. A reader who spends a few minutes on this page should leave with a defensible answer to each:

  • How has NYC’s for-hire transportation changed over the last decade? Has the rise of Uber and Lyft eaten into yellow cabs — and if so, how fast, and what happened to the green Boro Taxis that were introduced in 2013 to fix outer-borough access?
  • What does a normal week look like? When are the genuine rush hours, how late does Saturday night actually run, and do app-based services have a different weekly rhythm than yellow cabs?
  • Where in the city do pickups concentrate, hour by hour? Does taxi activity move the way we’d intuitively expect — flowing into Midtown in the morning, out to residential boroughs at night — or are there surprises hiding in the map?

These questions matter beyond curiosity. NYC just launched the country’s first congestion-pricing program in January 2025, with per-trip surcharges that differ for medallion taxis ($0.75) and high-volume ride-hail ($1.50). Understanding what the pre-pricing baseline looked like — who served whom, when, and where — is what makes it possible to ask whether the policy changed anything once a few years of post-2025 data are out.

Meet the four vehicle categories

The TLC dataset splits trips into four categories, and the differences between them are not just cosmetic — they are licensing categories with different legal rights about where they can pick up passengers. Knowing which is which is essential to reading any of the charts below. We use the same color for each category across all three visualizations.

Yellow medallion taxis

The iconic NYC yellow cab. There are roughly 13,500 medallions in circulation, a number capped by the city, and these are the only vehicles legally allowed to pick up street hails anywhere in the five boroughs — including all of Manhattan below 96th Street and the airports. Historically the TLC found that about 95% of yellow-cab pickups happened in the Manhattan core or at JFK/LaGuardia, which is exactly the imbalance the green Boro Taxi program was created to fix. Yellow cabs are the deepest historical record in the dataset, going back to 2009.

Green “Boro” taxis (street-hail liveries)

Apple-green cabs introduced in August 2013 under the Five Borough Taxi Plan. They are allowed to accept street hails only in upper Manhattan (above East 96th / West 110th Street) and in the four outer boroughs — not in the Manhattan core, and not at the airports for street pickups. They had the same meter rates as yellow cabs and were meant to bring legal, regulated taxi service to neighborhoods that yellow cabs rarely visited. The fleet peaked at around 7,500 active drivers in 2015 and has shrunk dramatically since — an early casualty of the rise of app-based ride-hail. Their decline is one of the clearest stories the data tells.

High-Volume For-Hire Vehicles (HVFHV)

The TLC’s name for app-dispatched ride-hail. Created as a separate reporting category in February 2019, it covers four licensed companies, identified by license codes: HV0003 Uber, HV0005 Lyft, HV0004 Via, and HV0002 Juno (Via and Juno are no longer operating, leaving Uber and Lyft as essentially the entire category today). HVFHV trips cannot be street-hailed; they have to be booked through an app. This category went from a sliver of the for-hire market in 2015 to the dominant share by the early 2020s — the single biggest structural change in NYC ground transportation in our lifetimes.

Other for-hire vehicles (FHV)

Traditional black cars, livery sedans, luxury limousines, and dispatch-only car services. Pre-arranged only — no street hails. Before 2019 this single dataset also contained all the early Uber and Lyft trips, which is why disentangling pre-2019 ride-hail volume from the rest of the FHV file is the trickiest piece of our preprocessing pipeline.

Dataset

We will use trip data from the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC Trip Record Data). This dataset includes detailed information on taxi and for-hire vehicle trips in New York City, such as pickup time, location, and type of vehicle. The dataset is large and fairly complex, with millions of trips recorded each month over multiple years. Since each trip has many details, we need to aggregate the data to make it readable and meaningful. It also becomes more complex when comparing different vehicle types and looking at changes over time, especially before and after the congestion pricing policy. These factors make this dataset a good fit for an interactive visualization that can help reveal patterns that wouldn’t be obvious in raw data.

Design decisions

Three time scales, three charts

The single hardest call early on was whether to build one ambitious view or several smaller, focused ones. We chose three because the dataset answers three different questions at three different time scales, and stretching a single chart across all of them would force us to either average out the weekly patterns (the choropleth would just show a generic Manhattan blob) or hide the decade-long market shift (which is invisible in any one day). The trade-off is that readers have to jump between views; the upside is each view is honest about what it’s showing.

One color palette, four meanings

Yellow (#f0a500), green (#4caf7d), HVFHV blue (#3d5a99), and gray (#aaaaaa) for other-FHV are reused identically across all three visualizations. Yellow and green are the obvious choices — they match the actual cabs. Blue for HVFHV reads as “tech / app” without being a literal Uber or Lyft brand color, which we wanted to avoid because the category includes both. Gray is intentionally muted: “other FHV” is a residual category and we don’t want it competing visually with the three categories the reader actually cares about.

Why a Wednesday in October for the choropleth

The map shows a single 24-hour period: Wednesday, October 16, 2024. We picked it deliberately. It is mid-week (no Monday or Friday weekend-tail effects), mid-fall (schools in session, no major holiday nearby, no severe weather in the historical record), and recent enough to reflect the post-pandemic, ride-hail-dominated city, but before congestion pricing took effect on January 5, 2025. In other words: as close to a “typical” modern NYC day as we can pick.

A full month for the heatmap, a multi-year span for market share

The hour×day heatmap aggregates a whole month so that no single weather event or news day dominates a cell, and so every (day, hour) bucket has enough trips to be a stable estimate. The market-share chart, by contrast, is intentionally coarse — one row per year per vehicle type. Anything finer would just add noise to a story that is fundamentally about a slow, decade-long structural shift.

Sequential color for counts, categorical color for types

Inside any one visualization where we are encoding a single quantity (trip counts on the choropleth, trip counts on the heatmap), we use a sequential color ramp from light to dark so brighter cells unambiguously mean more trips. The four-color categorical palette only appears where we are comparing vehicle types (the market-share chart and the type selector on the heatmap). Mixing the two encodings in the same view would be confusing.

City Wake Up: Total Trips by Hour and Day of Week

3. A decade of market share — stacked bars and donut (Ivan)

Each year from the start of the modern dataset to the most recent complete year, broken down by the four vehicle categories. The donut on top shows a single selected year in detail; the stacked bars below show the full sweep, including the 2020 COVID collapse and the steady erosion of yellow-cab share by HVFHV.

Green taxis began operating in NYC in 2013. However, their market share has steadily declined since 2015, and they have never achieved popularity comparable to yellow taxis. The sharpest decline occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic: green taxi trips fell from 6.3 million in 2019 to 1.7 million in 2020, a 3.6x decrease. By 2023, green taxis accounted for only about 787,000 trips. According to Wikipedia, TLC data showed that only 891 green taxis were still operating by February 2023, and in May 2023, the TLC began phasing out the program. The growth of app-dispatched ride-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft largely replaced the market segment that green taxis were originally designed to serve.


Despite the rapid rise of ride-hailing services, the iconic yellow taxi has remained prevalent. Since 2020, yellow taxis have maintained a relatively stable market share of around 13%. Dispatch-only car services have also retained their position, fluctuating between roughly 6% and 8% market share since 2018. Unlike street-hail taxis, this sector was less disrupted by the expansion of high-volume for-hire vehicles (HVFHV).

Events of interest

  • The 2018 ride-hailing cap - a temporary pause on issuing new HFHV licenses to study congestion - did not significantly slow the growth of app-dispatched taxis, whose market share still increased by about 6%.
  • As expected, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a dramatic decline in total taxi trips, dropping from approximately 368 million trips in 2019 to 184 million in 2020.
  • The rise of HFHV services significantly disrupted the traditional taxi industry, reducing the market share of street-hail services by roughly 50% for yellow taxis and about 8% for green taxis.
